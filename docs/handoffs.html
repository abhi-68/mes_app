Thermal Corp MES

Where work changes hands

What each role does in order, and every point at which one person's action lands on somebody else's screen. Read from the code and confirmed by an end-to-end run that drives these screens as all three roles.

Each role, in order

Numbered because these really are sequences — one thing genuinely has to happen before the next. Where a step ends with a coloured bar, that is the moment it becomes somebody else's problem.

Admin

Runs once per product, then rarely. Everything the other two roles do is shaped by what happens here.

  1. 01
    Add the parts and the product
    Bought-in parts get an opening stock and, if wanted, a reorder point. Made-here parts get their own steps. Opening stock is booked as a receipt through the ledger, never written straight into a balance.
    /admin/products
  2. 02
    Lay out the process
    Ordered steps, each at a station with an expected time. This is the "different process for different products" requirement, and it is data — nobody ships a new version because a station moved.
    /admin/products/[id]
  3. 03
    Say what goes into it, and where
    Each part is tied to the step that consumes it. That placement is what keeps stock numbers believable — material leaves when the step that uses it starts, not when the order closes.
    /admin/products/[id]
  4. 04
    Raise the work order
    One action creates every step, spawns a separate order for every made-here component, and writes the dependency records that decide what may start when.
    /admin/work-orders
    Admin→every station in the routing steps appear in each worker's queue

Worker

Runs many times a day, at one station, on a tablet. Sees only work they can act on.

  1. 01
    Look at the queue
    Split three ways: running now, ready to start, and waiting — and every waiting card says what it is waiting on, by name. Not "not your turn".
    /my-station
  2. 02
    Start
    The clock starts and the step's material leaves stock, in one transaction. If the material cannot be issued, nothing happens at all — the step does not end up running against parts that were never drawn. Tapping again, or a colleague clocking on, does not draw it twice.
    Worker→Inventory an issue movement, visible to everyone
  3. 03
    Mark done
    Hours are recorded from the clock. Progress rolls up the sub-assembly tree weighted by expected minutes, so a two-hour step counts for more than a ten-minute one.
    Worker→the next station the step that was queued behind this one becomes startable
    Worker→the order that needs the part finishing a sub-assembly's last step allocates it to the parent and alerts that station
  4. 04
    Can't continue
    A reason from a fixed list plus their own words. Fixed lists are what make these countable later; the free text is what makes them actionable now.
    Worker→Supervisor an alert with the reason and the note, and the order goes on hold
  5. 05
    Log scrap or rework
    Quantity against a reason code, at the step where it happened.
    Worker→Supervisor counts toward the scrap and rework breakdown in reports

Supervisor

Runs a few times a day. Almost everything they do starts from something a worker did.

  1. 01
    Check what is held up
    Every step that cannot start, and the thing holding it — a sub-assembly that has not delivered, stock that is short, a quality hold. Steps merely queued behind their own predecessor are counted separately, so the handful that are genuinely stuck are not buried under the ones that are simply waiting their turn.
    /waiting
  2. 02
    Work the alerts
    Blocked steps arrive as they happen. Shortages, overruns and reorder points are worked out fresh each visit, so they clear themselves when the cause is fixed rather than sitting there once resolved.
    /alerts
  3. 03
    Unblock, or cover the station
    A supervisor may start, complete, block or unblock at any station — that is how absences get covered and jams get cleared.
    Supervisor→Worker the step returns to their queue and the alert closes itself
  4. 04
    Correct a time entry
    The original is never overwritten. A correction is a new record carrying who changed it, from what, to what, and why — which is exactly why a worker cannot make one.
    /timesheets
    Supervisor→Worker visible on the worker's own timesheet, with the supervisor's name on it
  5. 05
    Read the numbers
    Average time per step, per station and per worker against estimate, and a scrap and rework breakdown. All of it computed from the clock — nobody types a figure in.
    /reports
  6. 06
    Raise the next order
    A supervisor can raise work orders without an admin. They cannot change what a product is made of or how it is built.
    /admin/work-orders
    Supervisor→every station a fresh set of steps, exactly as when an admin raises one

Every connection, and how it travels

Three mechanisms carry work between people, and the difference matters. A queue entry waits to be looked at. A stored alert is a fact about a moment that someone can mark dealt with. A derived alert is simply true right now and disappears by itself when the cause is fixed — which is why it cannot be dismissed.

From → to When How it travels Where it lands
Admin → stations An order is released Queue Each station's own step list
Worker → next step A step is marked done Queue Alert The next step becomes startable; if nothing else is in its way, that station is told
Worker → the waiting order The last step of a sub-assembly is done Alert The part is allocated to the parent's requirement, and final assembly gets “ready to start”
Worker → Supervisor Can't continue Alert Supervisor's alerts and the overview; the order goes on hold
Worker → Supervisor Start and mark done Derived Timesheets, and the averages in reports
Worker → everyone Starting a step draws its material Derived Inventory on hand, and a shortage alert if it takes something below what is needed
Supervisor → Worker Unblock Queue Back in the queue; the blocked alert closes itself
Supervisor → Worker Acts at another station Queue That station's card shows the step already running
Supervisor → Worker Corrects a time entry Record The worker's timesheet, with who changed it and why
Stock → Supervisor Free stock reaches a reorder point Derived Alerts and the inventory screen
The clock → Supervisor A step passes twice its estimate Derived Alerts, until the step is finished or paused
Supervisor → Admin Needs a new product, station, person or reason code No path Nothing in the system carries this — it is a conversation

Handoffs that do not exist yet

Worth naming, because a diagram of what connects is only honest alongside what doesn't.

Nothing leaves the building

Every connection above lands on a screen inside the app. No email, no text, no display on the wall — so a supervisor who has gone home, or a worker not holding a tablet, is told nothing until they next open it.

Nobody can ask the admin for anything

A supervisor who needs a new reason code or a new station has no way to request it. The only path is to go and find the person.

Quality never reaches anyone

Inspection, holds and rework decisions exist in the engine and no screen calls them, so a failed part cannot be held from the app — and a hold is one of the three things that can stop a parent order.

An admin's edit reaches live orders

Changing a routing or a bill of materials affects orders already on the floor. A released order should carry a frozen copy; that is specified and not built.

The stations, routings and assembly sequence in the demonstration data are a best guess from Thermal Corporation's public Air Handlers catalogue, not their confirmed process. The handoffs above are real and were exercised end to end; the factory they describe has not been validated.
